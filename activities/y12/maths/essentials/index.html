A calculator returns results for the expression and settings you give it. Your first task is to translate a mathematical statement accurately: grouping, negative signs, fractions and angle units all matter. A long decimal display does not make an input or a model correct.

Estimate before calculating, enter brackets deliberately and keep enough precision for later steps. Distinguish a stored value from the rounded number shown in a written answer. For trigonometry, check radians or degrees; for a graph, check the viewing window and the domain before concluding that a feature is absent.

Connect it to everyday life and industry

If a model predicts about 200 people and a calculation returns 0.2, check units and scale before copying it. A calculator can support a decision, but the answer needs interpretation: what quantity was found, what units belong to it and whether it is sensible.

01 · Translate the expression before pressing keys

The displayed result belongs to the entered expression, not to what you intended to enter. Group a whole numerator or denominator with brackets, and distinguish a negative number from a subtraction operation. A fraction bar groups expressions above and below it.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

An expression gives a value; an equation asserts equality. In f(x), f names a function and x its input; the brackets do not mean multiply by f. A coefficient multiplies a variable, an exponent is a power, and a subscript labels a member or condition. A fraction bar groups its full numerator and denominator; bracket those groups before calculator entry.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. For (3 + 5)/(2 × 4), evaluate the grouped numerator and denominator: 8/8 = 1.
  2. Entering 3 + 5/2 × 4 describes a different expression.
  3. Estimate the size first so an unexpected result prompts an input check.
02 · Control angle units, domain and window

Degrees divide a turn into 360 parts; radians relate arc length to radius. A trigonometric input must match the calculator mode. A graph window only shows part of a function: a missing intersection may lie outside it or outside a permitted domain.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

The domain is the allowed input set and range the resulting output set. An interval a ≤ x ≤ b includes endpoints a,b; < excludes an endpoint. A graph window specifies displayed x and y ranges, not the function’s domain. One full turn is 360° = 2π radians; π ≈ 3.14159. Trigonometric answers depend on the angle mode.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. sin(30°) = 0.5.
  2. sin(π/6 radians) = 0.5, but sin(30 radians) is a different value.
  3. Before interpreting a graph of √x, remember its real-number domain x ≥ 0.
03 · Keep precision until the final interpretation

Rounding an intermediate value can change later calculations. Store or reuse unrounded values where practical, then round the final answer according to the question and context. Exact forms such as fractions or radicals retain information that a short decimal may omit.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Exact values keep fractions, radicals such as √2, or constants such as π. An approximation is a rounded value, written ≈. Significant figures start at the first nonzero digit and describe retained precision; decimal places count digits after the point. Keep unrounded intermediate values and attach context units to the final result.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Use 1/3 + 1/3 + 1/3 = 1 rather than 0.33 + 0.33 + 0.33 = 0.99.
  2. A required minimum count of buses is rounded upward, not just to the nearest integer.
  3. Include units and explain what the result represents.
