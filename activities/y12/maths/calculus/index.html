Calculus studies change and accumulation. A derivative describes an instantaneous rate of change, which can be approached through slopes over smaller intervals. A definite integral describes signed accumulation over an interval. The units depend on the original function and the variable of integration.

A numerical derivative or integral is an approximation under a method and settings, not an automatic exact proof. A stationary point has derivative zero, but further reasoning is needed to classify it. Signed area below an axis contributes negatively; total geometric area or total distance can require splitting intervals and using magnitudes.

Connect it to everyday life and industry

If velocity is given in metres per second as a function of seconds, its definite integral gives displacement in metres. To find total distance, account for changes of direction. If a cost function is differentiated with respect to production quantity, the derivative has the interpretation and units of marginal cost.

01 · From average rate to instantaneous rate

An average rate compares output change with input change over an interval. A derivative is the limiting rate as that interval shrinks, when the limit exists. On a graph it is a tangent slope, not the height of the graph at the same input.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Average rate between x₁,x₂ is [f(x₂) − f(x₁)]/(x₂ − x₁). Derivative f′(x) or dy/dx is the limiting instantaneous rate, the tangent gradient. If x is seconds and y metres, dy/dx has units m/s. The prime is notation for differentiation, not an exponent; Δ denotes finite change, whereas d notation expresses a limit.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. For s(t) = t², the average rate from 2 to 2+h is ((2+h)² − 4)/h = 4 + h.
  2. As h approaches zero, the rate approaches 4.
  3. If s is metres and t seconds, this derivative is 4 m/s at t = 2 s.
02 · Classify a stationary point

Solving f′(x) = 0 identifies stationary candidates, but zero derivative does not by itself guarantee a maximum or minimum. Examine how the derivative changes sign or use a suitable second-derivative argument. Also inspect endpoints when finding an interval’s global extreme.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A stationary point has f′(x) = 0; it may be a maximum, minimum or neither. f″(x) is the second derivative, the rate of change of gradient. f″ > 0 at a stationary point indicates a local minimum; f″ < 0 a local maximum; f″ = 0 is inconclusive. Local means compared with nearby inputs, not necessarily the whole domain.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. For f(x) = x², f′(x) = 2x is negative before 0 and positive after 0, so x = 0 is a minimum.
  2. For g(x) = x³, g′(0) = 0 but the function continues increasing through 0.
  3. A calculator’s stationary result still needs classification.
03 · Distinguish accumulation, area and distance

A definite integral gives signed accumulation. Contributions below the axis have negative sign, so a net result can be smaller than total geometric area. For velocity, integrate signed values for displacement and magnitudes for total distance. Split at sign changes when needed.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx is a definite integral: signed accumulation between lower bound a and upper bound b; dx identifies integration variable x. If F′ = f, the value is F(b) − F(a). Negative graph regions subtract in signed area. For velocity v(t), ∫v(t)dt gives displacement, while ∫|v(t)|dt gives total distance; | | means absolute value.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. For v(t) = t − 2 on 0 ≤ t ≤ 4, negative and positive triangular areas cancel: displacement is 0.
  2. Each triangle has area 2, so total distance is 4 when units are m/s and seconds.
  3. Zero displacement does not mean the object stayed still.
