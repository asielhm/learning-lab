Forces and dynamics
Preparation and worked examples

A force is an interaction acting on a chosen object. Draw only those interactions in its free-body diagram, not a force called motion. Weight is gravitational force, mass measures inertia, and contact forces depend on the surroundings. Newton’s third-law partners act on different objects.

Acceleration follows the vector sum of forces, Fnet = ma. Zero net force means constant velocity, which can be nonzero. Static friction adjusts up to a limit, while kinetic friction models sliding. On an incline, resolve forces relative to the surface. For connected bodies, consistent system boundaries and shared motion constraints help organise the equations.

A car travelling at steady speed can have a nonzero driving force balanced by resistance. During braking it may still move forward while its acceleration points backward. A 5 kg body with 20 N right, 5 N left and 5 N friction left has a 10 N net force and 2 m/s² acceleration right.

  1. Read and explain. Learn the vocabulary and say why the process works.
  2. Work through an example. Identify the known information, rule and result.
  3. Investigate, then apply. Return to the practical, game or revision simulator.

Read the symbols and units

Use this reference whenever a letter, subscript or unit in an example is new to you. A subscript is a smaller label, such as ₁ for the first state; an exponent is a power, such as ² for a square.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Newton’s first law says zero net force means constant velocity. Second law ΣF = ma relates net force (N) to mass m (kg) and acceleration a (m/s²); Σ means sum with directions. Third-law pairs are equal and opposite forces acting on different bodies. They do not cancel on one body’s force diagram.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Mass m measures inertia in kg; weight W = mg is gravitational force in N, with g ≈ 9.81 m/s² near Earth. A free-body diagram shows forces on one selected object. A normal force is perpendicular contact force; tension is pull along a taut rope. “Normal” means perpendicular, not “usual”.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Static friction fs adjusts to prevent sliding, up to fs ≤ μsN. Kinetic friction fk = μkN models sliding. N is normal force, not mass; μs,μk are dimensionless coefficients. Friction opposes actual or impending relative sliding at contact, which is not always opposite the object’s overall travel direction.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

On a slope of angle θ above horizontal, weight components are mg sinθ down the plane and mg cosθ into it. m is mass, g gravity; sin and cos select components. N = mg cosθ only if no other perpendicular forces or acceleration act. Resolve forces along perpendicular axes before applying ΣF = ma.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Connected bodies share motion constraints from a taut, ideal rope. T is tension (N); m₁,m₂ are separate masses; a is their related acceleration. Draw each body’s forces before combining equations. A massless rope over a frictionless pulley has equal tension on both sides; a real pulley or rope may invalidate that assumption.

04

Friction

Understand the ideas

  • Static friction prevents relative sliding when within its limit.
  • Actual static friction can be smaller than its limiting value.
  • Static friction balances the applied force up to its limit.
  • Its direction resists relative sliding at the contact.
  • Using one coefficient is a simplifying assumption, not a universal material property.
  • First compare the pull with μN. Static friction balances it below the limit; otherwise use F_net = pull − μmg, then a = F_net/m. Result: 0 m/s².

Reasoning: For the combined system, external driving force is m₂g and total mass is m₁ + m₂. Thus a = m₂g/(m₁ + m₂). Result: 0.892 m/s².

Explain the reasoning in your own words, then compare it with the practical model. For a calculation, identify the known quantities and units before choosing the rule.

Force Bureau · concepts and examples

Find the net force and acceleration.

Use the vector sum of forces in Fnet = ma. Zero net force means constant velocity, not necessarily rest.

Compare one model solution

5 kg, net force 10 N right → 2 m/s² right; 4 kg, 12 N right and 4 N left → 2 m/s² right, with 8 N net; 2 kg, zero net force → 0 m/s²; 3 kg, net force 9 N left → 3 m/s² left

Identify the force.

Draw only forces acting ON the selected object. Its motion is not an extra force.

Compare one model solution

Earth pulling on a falling ball → Weight; Table pushing on a book → Normal contact force; Rope pulling a hanging load → Tension; Rough surface opposing sliding → Kinetic friction

Are the two forces a Newton third-law pair?

A third-law pair acts on two different objects. Forces on the same object can balance but are not a third-law pair.

Compare one model solution

Earth on book; book on Earth → Third-law pair; Weight on book; table on book → Not a third-law pair; Hand on wall; wall on hand → Third-law pair; Rope on load; load on rope → Third-law pair; Two opposing pushes on the same trolley → Not a third-law pair; Normal force and friction on the same block → Not a third-law pair

Tugboat Targets · concepts and examples

Mass 5 kg, sliding right. Friction is 5 N left. Make acceleration 2 m/s² right.

20 − 5 − 5 = 10 N; a = 10/5 = 2 m/s² right. Other pull pairs with the same difference also work.

Compare one model solution

Pull left / N: 5; Pull right / N: 20

Mass 10 kg, sliding right. Friction is 10 N left. Make acceleration 1 m/s² right.

25 − 5 − 10 = 10 N and a = 1 m/s². This is an instantaneous sliding-right model.

Mass 5 kg, sliding right. Friction is 10 N left. Make acceleration 2 m/s² left.

10 − 10 − 10 = −10 N, so a = −2 m/s². Velocity and acceleration need not point in the same direction.
