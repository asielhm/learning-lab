Metals
Preparation and worked examples

An element contains atoms with one proton number; a compound contains elements chemically combined in a defined composition. Metals have structures and bonding that help explain properties such as electrical conduction and malleability. A shiny appearance alone is not a definition of a metal.

In a simplified reactivity series, a more reactive metal more readily loses electrons in the compared reactions. It can displace a less reactive metal from appropriate salt solutions. The medium, temperature and protective oxide layers can change observations. Extracting a metal from its compound requires a suitable chemical or electrochemical process; a more reactive metal is often harder to obtain from a stable compound.

Iron production uses reduction of iron oxides, while aluminium production needs electrolysis of an appropriate molten electrolyte. Alloys combine useful properties: steel can be made stronger through composition and structure. An alloy is not simply a new element with its own single proton number.

Read the symbols and units

Use this reference whenever a letter, subscript or unit in an example is new to you. A subscript is a smaller label, such as ₁ for the first state; an exponent is a power, such as ² for a square.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

An atom is one unit of an element; an element contains one proton-number type. A molecule is a discrete group of bonded atoms. A compound contains elements chemically combined in fixed proportions. Not every compound consists of molecules: an ionic crystal is an extended lattice. A mixture combines substances without a fixed chemical formula.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Metallic bonding is attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons, which are not restricted to one atom. Electrical conductivity is ability to carry charge; malleable means can be hammered into shape; ductile means can be drawn into wire. Moving electrons explain conduction, while non-directional bonding allows layers to move without fully breaking attraction.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A group is a periodic-table column and a period a row. Valence electrons are outer-shell electrons important in bonding. Down Group 1, the outer electron is farther from the nucleus and more shielded by inner electrons, so it is generally lost more easily. This explains greater reactivity for these metals; halogens react by gaining electrons and have a different trend.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A cation has positive charge from losing electrons; an anion has negative charge from gaining them. In MgCl₂, Mg²⁺ and two Cl⁻ give +2 + 2(−1) = 0. The subscript 2 counts chloride ions in the ratio. Roman numerals in iron(III) indicate oxidation state +3, not three iron atoms.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

The reactivity series orders metals by their tendency to lose electrons under comparable conditions. A metal above hydrogen can often release H₂ from dilute non-oxidising acid, but oxide coatings and reaction conditions matter. A faster visible reaction can also result from greater surface area, higher concentration or higher temperature.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Displacement means one element replaces another in a compound. In Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, zinc loses two electrons (oxidation) and copper ions gain them (reduction). The charges superscripted on ions are electrical charges; coefficients needed for balancing electrons must not change the formulas.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

An ore contains enough useful material to justify extraction economically. Reduction removes oxygen or supplies electrons to a metal species. Carbon can reduce oxides of some less-reactive metals; very reactive metals often require electrolysis, decomposition driven by electricity. A blast furnace uses hot gases and carbon monoxide to reduce iron oxides, while limestone helps form removable slag.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

An alloy is a metallic mixture designed for useful properties. Different-sized atoms disrupt regular layers and can make sliding harder, increasing hardness. Corrosion is chemical deterioration by the environment; rusting specifically involves iron, oxygen and water. An alloy’s composition, structure and treatment all affect its behaviour.

Atoms elements and compounds

Understand the ideas

  • An element is defined by atomic type, not sample size or colour.
  • Water has different elements chemically bonded in a fixed composition.
  • O₂ contains only oxygen atoms in discrete molecules.
  • NaCl describes the ratio of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in the lattice.
  • Metallic bonding involves positive metal centres and delocalised electrons.
  • Brass is an alloy mixture of copper and zinc, not a fixed-formula compound.
  • Compounds have defined chemical composition; mixtures have variable composition.
  • Graphite contains carbon only, in extended covalent layers.
  • O₂ is a molecular element and H₂O a molecular compound, unlike metallic Cu and ionic NaCl.
  • Physical mixing does not by itself create a fixed-ratio compound.
