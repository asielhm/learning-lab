Organic chemistry connects carbon structures with properties and transformations. Carbon commonly forms four bonds in the simple models used here. Read the complete structure and functional group: a molecular formula counts atoms but does not uniquely identify how they are joined.

An open-chain alkane has only single carbon–carbon bonds; an alkene has a C=C bond, and a diene has two. Distillation separates molecules by boiling behaviour; cracking changes bonds and produces new molecules while conserving atoms. A functional group such as –OH or –COOH can determine important patterns of reactivity, but the rest of the molecule also matters.

Connect it to everyday life and industry

Refineries separate crude oil into fractions with boiling ranges and use reactions to obtain useful products. Polymers form long chains from smaller units. For the illustrative balance C₈H₁₈ → C₄H₆ + C₄H₁₀ + H₂, count 8 carbons and 18 hydrogens on each side; this is not a promise of a single industrial cracking product.

01 · Combustion and its products

Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon forms CO₂ and H₂O. Limited oxygen can produce CO and/or soot as well as water and some CO₂. A candle’s yellow colour alone is not a reliable measure of complete combustion.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A hydrocarbon contains only carbon and hydrogen. Complete combustion with sufficient oxygen forms CO₂ and H₂O. Incomplete combustion can form CO or carbon soot. CO₂ is carbon dioxide; CO is poisonous carbon monoxide. A balanced equation conserves each atom type by changing coefficients before formulas, not subscripts inside them.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Anhydrous CuSO₄ turns white to blue in the presence of water; CO₂ turns limewater milky.
02 · Crude oil and fractional distillation

Crude oil is a mixture, mainly of hydrocarbons. Fractional distillation separates by boiling ranges: vapours rise through a hot-bottom, cool-top column. Smaller alkanes tend to be more volatile, less viscous and easier to ignite than larger ones.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons. Fractional distillation separates ranges of boiling points: vaporised material rises and condenses where the column is cool enough. A fraction is a mixture with a boiling range, not a single pure compound. Condensation changes gas to liquid without cracking chemical bonds.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. A component condenses where the local column temperature falls to its boiling point. Fractions are mixtures with overlapping boiling ranges; distillation does not crack C–C bonds.
03 · Alkanes, alkenes and dienes

Acyclic alkanes have CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ and only single carbon–carbon bonds. Acyclic alkenes with one C=C have CₙH₂ₙ. An acyclic diene has two C=C bonds in the same chain and formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂. These formulas assume no rings.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

An alkane is a saturated hydrocarbon with single carbon–carbon bonds; an alkene has a C=C double bond. A diene has two C=C bonds in the same chain. For open-chain structures, CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ describes alkanes, CₙH₂ₙ one-double-bond alkenes and CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ two-double-bond dienes: n counts carbon atoms; subscripts count atoms. Rings or other bonds change these rules.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Butane is C₄H₁₀, butene is C₄H₈ and buta-1,3-diene is C₄H₆: CH₂=CH–CH=CH₂. Carbon still forms four bonds in every case.
04 · Cracking, dehydrogenation and bromine water

Cracking breaks long hydrocarbons into shorter products, often including alkenes. The optional diene challenge models a cracking and dehydrogenation pathway that also releases H₂. This is one possible balanced channel; actual products depend on conditions and form a mixture. Alkenes decolourise bromine by addition under the comparison conditions.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Cracking breaks a larger hydrocarbon into smaller molecules, often with heat and a catalyst. Dehydrogenation removes hydrogen as H₂ while increasing unsaturation, the number of multiple bonds or rings. C₈H₁₈ → C₄H₆ + C₄H₁₀ + H₂ is one balanced illustrative outcome, not a guaranteed product mixture. Bromine water is decolourised by suitable addition reactions at C=C bonds.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Standard: C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄. Challenge: C₁₀H₂₂ → C₆H₁₄ + C₄H₆ + H₂. Both conserve 10 carbon atoms and 22 hydrogen atoms. Hydrogen production is not required in every cracking equation.
05 · Addition polymers

Alkene double bonds open and monomers join to form a carbon-chain polymer. All monomer atoms are retained and no small molecule is released. Repeat units include substituents; chain ends are normally omitted from the repeat-unit notation.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A monomer is a small molecule that can join into a polymer, a chain of repeating units. Addition polymerisation opens C=C double bonds and joins monomers without releasing a small molecule. n before a monomer means many molecules; [repeat unit]ₙ means the enclosed unit repeats n times. Continuation bonds show the chain extends beyond the drawn unit.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Ethene gives [–CH₂–CH₂–]ₙ. Propene gives [–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–]ₙ; the CH₃ group stays as a side group.
06 · Alcohols, carboxylic acids and esters

An alcohol contains an –OH group outside a carboxyl group. Carboxylic acids contain –COOH and are commonly weak acids. Esters contain –COO– and can form reversibly from an alcohol and carboxylic acid with acid catalysis.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A functional group is a recognisable atom arrangement responsible for characteristic reactions. Alcohols contain –OH; carboxylic acids –COOH; esters –COO– between carbon groups. Esterification combines an alcohol and acid with loss of water and is reversible. “R” in a general formula means the rest of an organic group, not electrical resistance.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O produces ethyl ethanoate. The alcohol supplies the alkyl part of the ester name.
07 · Amines and amides

Amines contain nitrogen bonded to carbon and/or hydrogen, with a lone pair that can accept H⁺. Amides have nitrogen directly attached to a carbonyl carbon. Lone-pair delocalisation makes amides much less basic than comparable amines.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Amines contain an amine nitrogen group, such as –NH₂ attached to carbon; amides contain nitrogen joined to a carbonyl, –CONH– or related substitutions. A carbonyl is C=O. Their different connectivity changes properties; similar names do not make amines and amides interchangeable.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. CH₃NH₂ is methylamine. CH₃CONH₂ is ethanamide. Do not identify every nitrogen-containing compound as an amine.
08 · Polyesters and polyamides

A diol plus a dicarboxylic acid can form a polyester; a diamine plus a dicarboxylic acid can form a polyamide. Two reactive groups per monomer allow chain growth. Water forms in these acid + alcohol/amine condensation routes.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A polyester has repeated ester links, –COO–; a polyamide repeated amide links, –CONH–. Condensation polymerisation joins monomers with two reactive ends while releasing small molecules such as water in the models shown. “Biodegradable” depends on structure and conditions; being a polymer alone does not establish how fast it breaks down.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Joining 2n bifunctional monomers into one finite linear chain makes 2n − 1 links and releases 2n − 1 waters. Textbook repeat-unit equations often neglect the two end groups.
