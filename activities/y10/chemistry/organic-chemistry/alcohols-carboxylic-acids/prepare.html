01

Reading structures and functional groups

Understand the ideas

  • One line represents one shared electron pair; two lines represent a double bond.
  • A subscript applies to the element immediately before it: H₃ means three H atoms.
  • Count two C, 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 H, and one O.
  • An alcohol hydroxyl is bonded within the molecule; the complete neighbouring structure matters.
  • The same carbon has a C=O double bond and a single bond to O–H.
  • C is double-bonded to O above and single-bonded to OH below.
  • C=O contributes two, C–OH one and the bond to the attached group one: 2 + 1 + 1 = 4.
  • A molecular formula counts atoms; a structural formula adds connectivity.
  • Read C=O and O–H together on the same carbon.
  • The drawing directions help reading; rotating a drawing does not change which atoms are bonded.

02

Naming alcohols and carboxylic acids

Understand the ideas

  • One carbon gives meth-, and the alcohol group gives –ol.
  • Two carbons give eth-; the alcohol ending is –ol.
  • Number from the OH end: three carbons, OH at carbon 1.
  • The three-carbon chain has OH on its middle carbon, carbon 2.
  • There are four carbons; numbering from OH gives the lowest locant, 1.
  • The carboxyl carbon is the only carbon: methanoic acid.
  • Count the CH₃ carbon and the COOH carbon: two.
  • There are three carbons including the carboxyl carbon.
  • Four carbon atoms give but-, followed by the carboxylic-acid ending –oic acid.
  • Ethanoic acid and acetic acid name the same compound. Sodium ethanoate is a salt, not unchanged ethanoic acid.

Carboxylic acids strength and concentration

Understand the ideas

  • Vinegar is an aqueous solution containing ethanoic acid.
  • Weak describes partial ionisation; dilute describes concentration.
  • Concentration concerns amount per stated amount or volume of solution.
  • Strength concerns ionisation; amount per solution volume concerns concentration.
  • The acid has donated H⁺, leaving the negatively charged ethanoate ion.
  • An ion is an atom or group with an electrical charge.
  • H⁺ is hydrated in water; the simplified equation omits that detail.
  • The book uses pH 7 as neutral for water at about 25 °C.
  • Weak does not specify a unique concentration or pH.
  • Weak is a description of acid behaviour; it is not one fixed ionisation percentage for every solution.

05

Acid reactions and observations

Understand the ideas

  • CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O.
  • Sodium ethanoate contains sodium ions and ethanoate ions.
  • Neutralisation names the reaction, not a universal final-pH guarantee.
  • The acid–hydrogencarbonate reaction gives a salt, water and CO₂.
  • Sodium hydrogencarbonate is NaHCO₃, commonly called baking soda.
  • Effervescence is gas formation; bubbles alone do not identify the gas.
  • Carbon dioxide turns limewater milky in the standard gas test.
  • A bonded alcohol OH is not the same as the OH in a carboxyl group.
  • The salt, water and CO₂ account for the atoms supplied by the acid and hydrogencarbonate.
  • Distinguish the observation, gas formation, from the interpretation, the gas identity.

Ethanol oxidation and comparing the families

Understand the ideas

  • CH₃CH₂OH + O₂ → CH₃COOH + H₂O.
  • The alcohol end becomes a carboxyl group; the two carbons are retained.
  • The book uses this introductory description for the ethanol conversion.
  • Do not confuse the different starting substances and conditions.
  • Follow the starting molecule and reaction conditions rather than just noticing bubbles.
  • The arrow describes possible reaction directions; it is not a statement of equal amounts.
  • The class follows a particular primary-alcohol conversion, not every possible alcohol reaction.
  • One O is in ethanol and two are in O₂: three in total.
  • The lesson builds structure → family → prediction → evidence → explanation.
  • The entire carboxyl arrangement, not OH in isolation, determines this classification.
