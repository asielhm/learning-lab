In this aqueous course, acids are described through production of H₃O⁺, often abbreviated H⁺, while soluble hydroxide bases provide OH⁻. A base need not be a soluble hydroxide. Indicators provide evidence about a solution under specified conditions; their colour is a measurement clue, not a complete description of every chemical present.

Strength concerns how far an acid or base ionises or reacts with water; concentration concerns how much was added per quantity of solution. Neutralisation compares reacting amounts, so begin with the balanced equation before comparing volumes. pH is logarithmic, and an indicator endpoint is an observed range rather than an exact definition of equivalence.

Connect it to everyday life and industry

Vinegar concentration can be investigated by titration with a base of known concentration. A solution with 0.0020 mol of a monoprotic acid needs 0.0020 mol of hydroxide for complete neutralisation, not an arbitrary equal volume. Salt preparation also requires choosing a method suited to solubility and the reactants.

01 · Indicators and the pH scale

At 25°C, neutral pure water has pH 7. Universal indicator estimates a broad pH range; a single indicator changes over its own transition range. Natural red-cabbage colours vary with the extract and conditions.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

An indicator changes colour over a range of acidity. pH is a logarithmic measure of hydrogen-ion activity: in dilute solutions pH ≈ −log₁₀[H₃O⁺], where brackets denote concentration in mol/L and H₃O⁺ is hydronium. One pH unit represents a factor of ten. Neutral water has pH about 7 at 25 °C; the neutral pH depends on temperature.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. An acidic sample may turn methyl orange red and phenolphthalein colourless. Different colours do not mean different sample pH values.
02 · Strength, concentration and dilution

A strong acid ionises essentially completely in dilute aqueous solution; a weak acid establishes an equilibrium. Concentration is amount per volume. A concentrated weak acid and a dilute strong acid cannot be ranked by the words strong and weak alone.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Strength describes how completely an acid or base ionises in water; concentration describes how much solute is present per solution volume. Dilution adds solvent, the dissolving medium. In c₁V₁ = c₂V₂, c is concentration, V solution volume and 1,2 label initial and final states. Use matching units and assume no solute reaction or loss.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. At equal 0.010 mol/L, ideal HCl has pH about 2; ethanoic acid has a higher pH because it is only partially ionised.
03 · Mass concentration, molarity and ppm

Molar concentration is n/V in mol/L. Mass concentration can be mg/L. For dilute water-based solutions of density near 1 kg/L, mg/L is approximately ppm by mass. Do not interchange ppm and mol/L without mass and molar-mass information.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Mass concentration = solute mass/solution volume, for example g/L. Molarity c = n/V uses n moles of solute and V litres of solution, giving mol/L. A mole contains 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities. n = m/M uses mass m in g and molar mass M in g/mol. ppm means parts per million; mg/L approximates mass ppm only for dilute water of density about 1 kg/L.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. 60 mg in 2 L gives 30 mg/L. Add 1 L of water: the same 60 mg is now in 3 L, giving 20 mg/L, about 20 ppm.
04 · Neutralisation and ionic equations

Acid + alkali gives salt and water. For a strong-acid / strong-alkali aqueous reaction, H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O removes the reacting ions and leaves spectators. Other bases such as oxides and carbonates need their own balanced equations.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Neutralisation reacts acid and base. In H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, H⁺ represents hydrated hydrogen ions and OH⁻ hydroxide; → means forms. An ionic equation shows species that change; spectator ions remain unchanged and cancel. At equivalence, reactants have met in the equation’s mole ratio; equal volumes or pH 7 are not universal equivalence rules.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ remain dissolved. Equal volumes reach equivalence here only when the concentrations and stoichiometry match.
05 · Name the salt from the acid and cation

Hydrochloric acid forms chlorides, nitric acid forms nitrates and sulfuric acid forms sulfates. The metal or ammonium ion supplies the first part of the salt name. Balance charges when writing the formula.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A salt is an ionic compound of positive cations and negative anions. The acid supplies the anion name: hydrochloric gives chloride, sulfuric sulfate and nitric nitrate. The metal or ammonium supplies the cation. Subscripts give the smallest whole-number ratio with total charge zero, not the charges themselves.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. H₂SO₄ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O. Two K⁺ ions balance one SO₄²⁻ ion.
06 · Acid + metal or carbonate

A metal above hydrogen can release H₂ from a suitable dilute non-oxidising acid. Carbonates give a salt, CO₂ and water. Acid identity and reaction conditions matter: copper does not produce H₂ from dilute HCl.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A reactive metal with a suitable acid can produce a salt and hydrogen, H₂. A carbonate contains CO₃²⁻; acid plus carbonate produces salt, water and carbon dioxide, CO₂. Coefficients before formulas count relative moles; subscripts count atoms within a formula. Effervescence means gas bubbles forming; it does not identify which gas without a test.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + CO₂ + H₂O. Try the gas-syringe comparison in Metals.
07 · Solubility and salt preparation

All common nitrates and Group 1 / ammonium salts are soluble. Most chlorides are soluble, with important exceptions such as AgCl and PbCl₂; BaSO₄ and PbSO₄ are insoluble, while CaSO₄ is sparingly soluble. Most carbonates are insoluble except Group 1 and ammonium carbonates.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Solubility is the amount that dissolves at a stated temperature; a saturated solution is at its solubility limit. A precipitate is an insoluble solid formed from a solution. Filtration separates solid from liquid; crystallisation forms an ordered solid from a concentrated solution. Excess solid means more than needed to react, not a more concentrated dissolved solution.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. To make a soluble salt from an insoluble base: use excess base, filter, concentrate and cool. To make an insoluble salt: mix soluble sources, filter the precipitate, wash and dry it.
08 · Vinegar, Epsom salts and everyday chemistry

A vinegar titre gives the concentration in the sample actually titrated; apply any stated dilution factor afterwards. Epsom salt is MgSO₄·7H₂O. Baking powder contains acid salts and bicarbonate; cream of tartar is an acid salt. Soap stabilises oil droplets by amphiphilic ions.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Titration uses a measured volume of one solution to find the concentration of another. For a 1:1 reaction, caVa = cbVb: c is molar concentration, V volume and a,b identify acid and base. Other mole ratios require coefficients. A burette delivers measured liquid; the endpoint is an indicator change and should closely track equivalence. Hydrated salts contain water in their crystal formula.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. 25.0 mL of diluted vinegar neutralises 20.0 mL of 0.100 mol/L NaOH. With a 1:1 reaction, c = 0.100 × 20.0 / 25.0 = 0.0800 mol/L.
