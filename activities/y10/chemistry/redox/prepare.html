Redox
Preparation and worked examples

Redox describes paired changes involving electrons. Oxidation is electron loss and reduction is electron gain. Oxidation states help keep accounts even when electrons are not literally transferred as isolated particles. A half-equation must conserve both atoms and charge.

An electrolytic cell uses an external source to drive a reaction. Reduction occurs at the cathode and oxidation at the anode; in electrolysis the cathode is negative. Ions move through the electrolyte and electrons through metallic wires. Molten salts and aqueous solutions can produce different substances because water introduces competing reactions.

Electroplating deposits metal by reducing ions onto a surface. The charge passed is current multiplied by time, but how much metal appears also depends on electrons per ion and efficiency. Rusting is another redox context: the metal oxidation is paired with a reduction process in its surroundings.

  1. Read and explain. Learn the vocabulary and say why the process works.
  2. Work through an example. Identify the known information, rule and result.
  3. Investigate, then apply. Return to the practical, game or revision simulator.

Read the symbols and units

Use this reference whenever a letter, subscript or unit in an example is new to you. A subscript is a smaller label, such as ₁ for the first state; an exponent is a power, such as ² for a square.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Oxidation is loss of electrons and reduction is gain. They occur together because electrons transferred from one species must be received by another. A half-equation shows one part, for example Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. e⁻ is an electron; 2 counts electrons; ²⁺ is ion charge. Remember OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

An oxidising agent accepts electrons and is itself reduced; a reducing agent donates them and is oxidised. A half-equation must balance atoms and total charge. Multiply complete half-equations until electron counts match, then add and cancel electrons; never change a chemical subscript to balance charge.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Current I is charge passing per time: I = Q/t, with Q in coulombs (C), t seconds and I amperes (A). Electrons carry charge through metal wires; moving ions carry it through electrolyte. An electrolyte is a liquid or solution containing mobile ions. A solid ionic lattice does not usually conduct because its ions cannot move freely.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

The cathode is where reduction occurs; the anode where oxidation occurs. In an electrolytic cell the external DC supply makes the cathode negative and anode positive. Cations move towards the cathode and anions towards the anode. DC means direct current with a fixed direction; electrode names are defined by reaction, not a universal sign in all cells.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Aqueous means dissolved in water, written (aq). At each electrode, possible water reactions compete with dissolved-ion reactions. Product selection depends on species, concentration, electrode material and conditions. An inert electrode ideally conducts without being consumed; an active electrode can participate. Do not use the molten-salt rule blindly for water solutions.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Overall, 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ means two moles of water give two moles of hydrogen and one of oxygen. At equal temperature and pressure, their gas volumes have ratio 2:1. Hydrogen gives a small pop with a lit splint; oxygen relights a glowing splint. These are virtual demonstrations, not instructions to test accumulated gases without supervision.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Al³⁺ denotes an aluminium ion missing three electrons. Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al is cathodic reduction. Aluminium oxide is dissolved in molten cryolite to lower operating temperature and improve conduction. Molten means melted, not aqueous. Carbon anodes are consumed as oxygen-containing products form, so industrial extraction needs electrode replacement and energy.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Photography can reduce silver ions to silver metal, producing dark regions. Thermite uses aluminium to reduce iron(III) oxide: Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃. The coefficients balance atoms; oxidation states track electron transfer. Exothermic means energy is released to surroundings; the very high temperature makes thermite an industrial process requiring specialist controls.

Understand the ideas

  • Positive and negative ions move through the electrolyte.
  • Metallic wires conduct through mobile electrons.
  • Opposite charges attract; cations move towards the negative electrode.
  • Negative ions move towards the positive electrode.
  • Cathode names the electrode where reduction occurs.
  • Anode names the electrode where oxidation occurs.
  • Ion mobility changes; the solid already contains ions but they are fixed in the lattice.
  • Electrical energy drives the electrode reactions.
  • Electrolytic cathodes are negative; galvanic cathodes are positive.
  • Transport and electrode reaction are related but different processes.

Photography and thermite

Understand the ideas

  • Development reduces silver ions to visible silver metal.
  • The developer supplies reducing action that amplifies the latent image.
  • Fixing removes light-sensitive unexposed material and stabilises the image.
  • The light–dark relationship is reversed relative to the original subject.
  • Silver ions gain electrons, so the process is reduction.
  • Aluminium is sufficiently reactive to reduce the iron oxide in this comparison.
  • Two Al and two Fe atoms balance, along with three O atoms.
  • Al goes from 0 to +3, while Fe goes from +3 to 0.
  • Two Al atoms each lose three electrons, matching reduction of two Fe³⁺ centres.
  • A less reactive metal cannot perform the same oxygen-removal role under the stated conditions.
