Redox describes paired changes involving electrons. Oxidation is electron loss and reduction is electron gain. Oxidation states help keep accounts even when electrons are not literally transferred as isolated particles. A half-equation must conserve both atoms and charge.

An electrolytic cell uses an external source to drive a reaction. Reduction occurs at the cathode and oxidation at the anode; in electrolysis the cathode is negative. Ions move through the electrolyte and electrons through metallic wires. Molten salts and aqueous solutions can produce different substances because water introduces competing reactions.

Connect it to everyday life and industry

Electroplating deposits metal by reducing ions onto a surface. The charge passed is current multiplied by time, but how much metal appears also depends on electrons per ion and efficiency. Rusting is another redox context: the metal oxidation is paired with a reduction process in its surroundings.

01 · Oxidation and reduction

Oxidation is electron loss and an increase in oxidation state. Reduction is electron gain and a decrease in oxidation state. In simple oxygen-transfer reactions, gaining oxygen is oxidation and losing oxygen is reduction.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ is oxidation. Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu is reduction. Both changes occur in the same redox process.
02 · Agents and balanced half-equations

The reducing agent donates electrons and is oxidised; the oxidising agent accepts electrons and is reduced. Balance atoms and charge, then multiply half-equations so the electron numbers cancel.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. 2Al → 2Al³⁺ + 6e⁻ and 2Fe³⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Fe match the six-electron transfer in thermite.
03 · What carries charge?

An external metal circuit carries charge through electrons. An electrolyte carries charge through moving ions. Solid ionic compounds do not conduct by ion migration, but molten or aqueous electrolytes can.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Cations migrate to the negative cathode and anions to the positive anode in an electrolytic cell. Migration is not the same as electron transfer at the electrode surface.
04 · Electrode rules

Reduction always occurs at the cathode and oxidation at the anode. For electrolysis, the power supply makes the cathode negative and anode positive. Electrode names describe the reaction; polarity differs in a galvanic cell.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Molten PbBr₂: Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb at the cathode; 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ at the anode.
05 · Aqueous solutions: water competes

Do not apply molten-salt rules to every aqueous solution. Water can be reduced to H₂ or oxidised to O₂. Ion concentration, electrode material and overpotential affect which reactions occur.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Concentrated aqueous CuCl₂ with inert electrodes gives Cu at the cathode and usually Cl₂ at the anode; CuSO₄ with inert electrodes gives Cu and O₂. Copper anodes can dissolve instead.
06 · Water electrolysis and gas tests

Acidified water with inert electrodes gives H₂ and O₂ in a 2:1 mole and volume ratio at equal temperature and pressure. Hydrogen gives a pop with a burning splint; oxygen relights a glowing splint. Carbon dioxide turns limewater milky.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂. With the same charge, n(H₂) = Q/(2F) and n(O₂) = Q/(4F), assuming 100% current efficiency.
07 · Aluminium extraction

Alumina is dissolved in molten cryolite. Cryolite lowers the required operating temperature compared with pure alumina. Aluminium forms at the cathode; carbon anodes react to form carbon oxides and need replacing.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al. In the ideal CO₂-only anode model, 2Al₂O₃ + 3C → 4Al + 3CO₂.
08 · Photography and thermite

In silver-halide photography, exposure creates a latent image, development reduces silver ions to visible metallic silver, and fixing removes unexposed silver halide. Thermite is a separate highly exothermic oxygen-transfer redox reaction.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. 2Al + Fe₂O₃ → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe. Aluminium is oxidised; iron(III) is reduced. The photographic image forms by Ag⁺ gaining electrons, not by silver ions being oxidised.
