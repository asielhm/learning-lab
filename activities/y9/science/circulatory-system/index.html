Your cells need a continuous supply of oxygen and nutrients and a way to remove waste. Blood is the transport fluid, blood vessels provide routes, and the heart produces the pressure differences that keep it moving. The heart is a muscular organ, not a store of oxygen and not the place where air enters the body.

The circulation has two connected circuits. The right side sends blood to the lungs; the left side sends blood to the body. Atria receive blood and ventricles pump it out. Valves guide flow in one direction as pressure changes. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, while veins return it; those names describe direction, not oxygen content.

Connect it to everyday life and industry

When you exercise, muscles use oxygen faster and produce more carbon dioxide. Heart rate and blood flow can increase to support transport. The left ventricle has a thicker muscular wall because it normally generates higher pressure for the systemic circuit. Both ventricles must nevertheless move the same average volume over time in a stable circulation.

01 · Follow the double circuit

Blood returning from the body enters the right atrium, passes to the right ventricle and goes through pulmonary arteries to the lungs. It returns through pulmonary veins to the left atrium, enters the left ventricle and leaves through the aorta to the body. The two circuits are linked, not separate blood supplies.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A circuit is a complete route. Pulmonary means involving the lungs; systemic means involving the rest of the body. An artery carries blood away from the heart and a vein towards it. Oxygenated means relatively oxygen-rich, not oxygen-only. Deoxygenated blood still contains oxygen. The two circuits run in series, with blood passing through the heart twice per complete route.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Body → venae cavae → right atrium → right ventricle.
  2. Pulmonary arteries → lungs → pulmonary veins.
  3. Left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body.
02 · Understand chambers and valves

Atria receive blood at relatively low pressure; ventricles pump it into vessels. Valves open and close because of pressure differences and help prevent backward flow. The septum separates the two sides, which normally carry blood with different oxygen contents.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

An atrium is a receiving chamber; a ventricle pumps blood out. A valve is a flap arrangement that helps prevent reverse flow as pressures change. The septum is the wall separating left and right sides. Chordae tendineae are supporting cords attached to atrioventricular valves; they do not actively pull the valves open.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. During ventricular contraction, atrioventricular valves close.
  2. When ventricular pressure exceeds arterial pressure, outlet valves open.
  3. Valve movement is caused by changing pressure, not by a decision to pull blood through.
03 · Connect structure to transport

The left ventricle usually needs a thicker muscular wall to generate higher systemic pressure. Arteries have walls suited to their pressure conditions; capillaries form thin exchange surfaces; veins return blood at lower pressure, often aided by valves and body movement.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A capillary is a microscopic exchange vessel with a thin wall. Diffusion is net movement down a concentration gradient, from higher to lower concentration. A gradient is a difference across a distance. Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume: beats/min × mL/beat gives mL/min. Stroke volume is blood pumped by one ventricle per beat.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. A pulmonary artery still carries blood away from the heart, despite its lower oxygen content.
  2. Pulmonary veins return oxygenated blood from the lungs.
  3. Blood with lower oxygen is still red; diagram colours are a labelling convention.

YOUR FIRST TASK

Find the heart.

Your heart is about the size of your fist. It lies inside the thoracic cavity, protected by the rib cage.

Think about it

Is the heart completely on the left side of your chest?

No. The heart lies between both lungs, behind the sternum, with more of it to the left of the body's midline.
🔎 Observe: Notice that the heart is positioned between the two lungs, not inside the left lung.
