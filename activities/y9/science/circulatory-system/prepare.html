Circulatory system
Preparation and worked examples

Your cells need a continuous supply of oxygen and nutrients and a way to remove waste. Blood is the transport fluid, blood vessels provide routes, and the heart produces the pressure differences that keep it moving. The heart is a muscular organ, not a store of oxygen and not the place where air enters the body.

The circulation has two connected circuits. The right side sends blood to the lungs; the left side sends blood to the body. Atria receive blood and ventricles pump it out. Valves guide flow in one direction as pressure changes. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, while veins return it; those names describe direction, not oxygen content.

When you exercise, muscles use oxygen faster and produce more carbon dioxide. Heart rate and blood flow can increase to support transport. The left ventricle has a thicker muscular wall because it normally generates higher pressure for the systemic circuit. Both ventricles must nevertheless move the same average volume over time in a stable circulation.

Read the symbols and units

Use this reference whenever a letter, subscript or unit in an example is new to you. A subscript is a smaller label, such as ₁ for the first state; an exponent is a power, such as ² for a square.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A circuit is a complete route. Pulmonary means involving the lungs; systemic means involving the rest of the body. An artery carries blood away from the heart and a vein towards it. Oxygenated means relatively oxygen-rich, not oxygen-only. Deoxygenated blood still contains oxygen. The two circuits run in series, with blood passing through the heart twice per complete route.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

An atrium is a receiving chamber; a ventricle pumps blood out. A valve is a flap arrangement that helps prevent reverse flow as pressures change. The septum is the wall separating left and right sides. Chordae tendineae are supporting cords attached to atrioventricular valves; they do not actively pull the valves open.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A capillary is a microscopic exchange vessel with a thin wall. Diffusion is net movement down a concentration gradient, from higher to lower concentration. A gradient is a difference across a distance. Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume: beats/min × mL/beat gives mL/min. Stroke volume is blood pumped by one ventricle per beat.

01

Heart structure

Understand the ideas

  • The venae cavae return blood from the body to the right atrium. The left atrium receives blood from the lungs.
  • The left ventricle contracts to send blood through the aorta to the body.
  • The septum is the muscular partition between the ventricles. It prevents direct mixing of their blood.
  • The atria receive blood: the right atrium from the body and the left atrium from the lungs.
  • The left ventricle pumps through systemic circulation, which requires greater pressure than the shorter pulmonary circuit.
  • The apex is the pointed inferior tip of the heart and is formed mainly by the left ventricle.
  • Heart muscle needs its own blood supply. Coronary arteries branch from the aorta to supply it.
  • Anatomical left and right belong to the specimen, as if it were facing the viewer.
  • A defect in the ventricular septum can allow blood to pass between ventricles. The direction depends on the pressures on each side.
  • Diffusion from chamber blood is insufficient to supply the whole thick myocardium; coronary vessels deliver blood to the muscle itself.

02

Blood flow and vessels

Understand the ideas

  • The pulmonary trunk leaves the right ventricle and divides into pulmonary arteries leading to the lungs.
  • Pulmonary veins carry oxygen-rich blood from the lungs to the left atrium.
  • Arteries are defined by direction of flow. Pulmonary arteries carry oxygen-poor blood away from the heart.
  • Veins return blood towards the heart. Pulmonary veins are an important example carrying oxygen-rich blood.
  • Blood returning from the body enters the right atrium, passes into the right ventricle and travels towards the lungs.
  • Oxygen-rich blood returns from the lungs to the left side of the heart, which pumps it into the aorta.
  • The pulmonary circuit links heart and lungs; the systemic circuit links heart and body. One complete circuit therefore passes through the heart twice.
  • Capillaries have very thin walls and form networks close to cells, allowing exchange over short distances.
  • The pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood away from the right ventricle. Oxygen content alone does not define an artery.
  • Oxygen diffuses from capillary blood to respiring cells. Returning blood has less oxygen, although it still contains some.

03

Blood and exchange

Understand the ideas

  • Haemoglobin in red blood cells binds oxygen and transports most of the oxygen in blood.
  • Haemoglobin binds oxygen reversibly, loading it in the lungs and releasing it in tissues.
  • Platelets collect at damaged vessels and help trigger clotting, which reduces blood loss.
  • White blood cells form part of the immune system. Different types engulf pathogens or help make specific immune responses.
  • Plasma transports dissolved substances including glucose, hormones, urea and much of the carbon dioxide in blood.
  • The biconcave disc shape provides a high surface-area-to-volume ratio and a short path for diffusion.
  • Mature human red blood cells lack a nucleus, providing more space for oxygen-carrying haemoglobin.
  • Fewer red blood cells usually reduce the amount of haemoglobin available to transport oxygen.
  • Most carbon dioxide is transported as hydrogencarbonate in plasma, with smaller amounts dissolved or bound to proteins.
  • Thin capillary walls allow exchange with tissue fluid while blood normally stays within the vessel network.

04

Valves and pressure

Understand the ideas

  • Valves open and close in response to pressure differences, helping blood flow in the correct direction.
  • The tricuspid valve guards the right atrioventricular opening.
  • The mitral, or bicuspid, valve lies between the left atrium and left ventricle.
  • The aortic semilunar valve is at the outlet of the left ventricle.
  • The pulmonary valve guards the right ventricular outlet, resisting backflow from the pulmonary trunk.
  • A pressure difference pushes AV valve cusps closed as ventricular pressure rises.
  • The chordae attach AV valve cusps to papillary muscles, helping prevent prolapse during ventricular contraction.
  • Venous valves help maintain flow towards the heart, especially against gravity; skeletal muscle contractions also help move blood.
  • The mitral valve normally closes between left ventricle and left atrium. A leak can send some blood back into the left atrium.
  • Arteries carry blood at relatively high pressure. Elastic tissue stretches during pumping and recoils between beats.

05

Exercise and calculations

Understand the ideas

  • A faster heart rate helps deliver more oxygen and nutrients to active muscles and remove carbon dioxide.
  • Ventricular contraction produces a pressure wave in the arteries that can be felt as a pulse.
  • There are four 15-second intervals in one minute: 18 × 4 = 72 beats per minute.
  • Missing one beat in a 15-second count changes the estimate by 4 beats/min when multiplied by four. Missing one beat in a full-minute count changes it by only 1 beat/min. This comparison assumes a steady pulse.
  • Muscle activity increases energy demand. Blood supplies oxygen and glucose for aerobic respiration and removes products such as carbon dioxide.
  • Stroke volume is the volume ejected by one ventricle with each beat. It is different from heart rate.
  • Cardiac output is the volume pumped by one ventricle each minute, calculated from beats per minute multiplied by volume per beat.
  • 80 beats/min × 70 mL/beat = 5,600 mL/min, or 5.6 litres per minute.
  • At rest: 70 × 60 = 4,200 mL/min. During exercise: 140 × 90 = 12,600 mL/min. Both rate and stroke volume matter.
  • For the same cardiac output, a lower heart rate must be balanced by a larger volume pumped per beat.

06

Health and investigations

Understand the ideas

  • Narrowing reduces blood supply to heart muscle, limiting its oxygen and nutrient supply.
  • Atherosclerosis involves plaque build-up in arterial walls, which can narrow vessels and increase cardiovascular risk.
  • A blocked coronary artery can starve part of the myocardium of oxygen. Prolonged loss of supply can cause cell death.
  • Regular activity and avoiding smoking are evidence-based ways to lower cardiovascular risk, alongside other healthy habits.
  • Persistently high blood pressure can damage vessel walls and increases the workload of the heart.
  • The independent variable is exercise duration. Keeping other important conditions similar helps isolate its effect.
  • Heart rate often decreases as recovery proceeds. Standardising the measurement delay makes results more comparable.
  • Repeats and a mean reduce the influence of random variation. They do not remove a consistently biased measurement method.
  • One observation shows a change, but fitness, stress, temperature, illness and measurement conditions can all influence pulse.
  • A raw count depends on pulse rate and counting duration. Convert counts to beats per minute and standardise other important conditions so that the comparison can isolate the intended exercise variable.
