01

Current and charge flow

Understand the ideas

  • Current is charge transferred per time: I = Q/t. Its unit is the ampere, A.
  • One ampere is one coulomb per second: 1 A = 1 C/s.
  • A complete loop permits sustained current. Mobile charges were present throughout the metal before closure.
  • Charge does not accumulate indefinitely in a steady series loop. Energy transfers, while the same charge rate continues.
  • I = Q/t = 6 C ÷ 3 s = 2 C/s = 2 A.
  • Q = It = 0.5 A × 8 s = 4 C. The circuit does not use up this charge.
  • Conventional current follows positive-charge flow. Metal electrons drift in the opposite direction.
  • Mobile electrons carry the net drift current; positive ion cores remain in the lattice.
  • In a steady junction, entering current equals leaving current. The remaining branch current is 0.7 − 0.2 = 0.5 A.
  • Individual drift and electrical signal propagation are different. The current involves mobile charges already present around the circuit.

02

Potential difference and energy

Understand the ideas

  • V = E/Q. Voltage compares two points and is measured in joules per coulomb, or volts.
  • The volt is a joule per coulomb: 1 V = 1 J/C. It is not itself a flow rate.
  • The shop supplies pizzas as the cell supplies energy per charge. Couriers represent charge packets.
  • Energy transfers at the component; charge continues around a complete loop.
  • V = E/Q = 12 J ÷ 2 C = 6 V.
  • E = VQ = 3 V × 4 C = 12 J.
  • Potential difference and charge-flow rate are distinct physical quantities with different units.
  • Series potential differences add to the supply voltage, neglecting wire and internal resistance: 6 − 2 = 4 V.
  • I = V/R doubles when V doubles at fixed R. Energy per coulomb is V and doubles too.
  • The analogy separates charge circulation from energy transfer. It does not describe the full electromagnetic mechanism.

03

Resistance and Ohm’s law

Understand the ideas

  • Resistance is measured in ohms. A, C and W measure current, charge and power respectively.
  • Rearrange V = IR by dividing both sides by R. Use volts and ohms to obtain amps.
  • Ohm’s law describes a constant-resistance relationship under unchanged physical conditions.
  • I = V/R. Increasing the denominator at fixed V reduces the steady current.
  • I = V/R = 6/20 = 0.30 A.
  • R = V/I = 4/0.20 = 20 Ω. Divide by 0.20, rather than multiply.
  • Direct proportion means the same multiplicative change in V and I when R is constant.
  • Heating changes the filament’s resistance, so it is not a constant-temperature ohmic resistor across its full range.
  • An ohmic test checks proportionality over several readings with temperature and conditions controlled.
  • The ratio at the origin is indeterminate. A constant-resistance line’s non-zero points or gradient provide the resistance.

04

Meters and current–voltage graphs

Understand the ideas

  • An ammeter measures current through a path. Its very low resistance makes a direct supply connection inappropriate.
  • A voltmeter compares two points across the measured component and has high resistance.
  • Our graph plots I vertically against V horizontally. Axis orientation matters when interpreting gradient.
  • Direct proportion means a straight line through (0,0) for I versus V.
  • Gradient is ΔI/ΔV. Since I = V/R, it equals reciprocal resistance, in A/V.
  • At a given voltage, lower resistance permits greater current, so I/V and the graph gradient are larger.
  • Changing resistor or temperature changes resistance and creates a different relationship, not another point on the same controlled test.
  • With V = RI, the gradient ΔV/ΔI equals resistance. Always read the axis labels before interpreting slope.
  • The I–V gradient is 0.20/4 = 0.05 A/V = 1/R, so R = 1/0.05 = 20 Ω.
  • An ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance. Real voltmeters have high, finite resistance, which can affect sensitive circuits.

05

Series and parallel circuits

Understand the ideas

  • Series means a single sequential path, so steady current is the same through both components.
  • Parallel branches share the same potential difference; their currents depend on their resistances.
  • For series resistors, R_total = R₁ + R₂ = 20 + 40 = 60 Ω.
  • Each branch connects across the same pair of junctions and therefore has the same voltage.
  • The total resistance is 60 Ω, so I = 6/60 = 0.10 A through both resistors.
  • That branch has the full 6 V, so I₂ = 6/40 = 0.15 A.
  • Branch currents are 6/20 = 0.30 A and 6/40 = 0.15 A. Their sum is 0.45 A.
  • The extra branch provides another conducting path. For positive branch resistances, equivalent resistance is lower than before.
  • R_total = R₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂) = 800/60 ≈ 13.33 Ω, less than either branch resistance.
  • V₁ = IR₁ = 0.10 × 20 = 2 V; V₂ = 0.10 × 40 = 4 V. The larger resistance takes the larger share of voltage.

06

Wire factors, power and applications

Understand the ideas

  • Resistivity is a material property in Ω m. Resistance also depends on geometry: R = ρL/A.
  • R is directly proportional to L for a uniform wire at fixed resistivity and area.
  • R = ρL/A. A larger conducting area provides more parallel paths and lowers resistance.
  • P = E/t, measured in watts, where 1 W = 1 J/s. For a circuit component, P = VI.
  • P = VI = 6 × 0.30 = 1.8 W.
  • E = Pt = 2 W × 10 s = 20 J.
  • These approximate metal models have positive temperature coefficients. Other materials and temperature ranges can behave differently.
  • Area A = π(d/2)² grows by a factor of four, so R = ρL/A falls to one quarter.
  • P = VI, so higher V permits lower I at the same P. Cable heating depends on I²R; the resistance need not decrease.
  • At fixed V, P = V²/R, so doubling R halves total power. The statement P = I²R must be used with the actual changed current, not a falsely fixed I.
