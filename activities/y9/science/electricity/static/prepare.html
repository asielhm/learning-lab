01

Atoms and charge

Understand the ideas

  • Electrons are negative. Protons are positive and neutrons have no electric charge.
  • The nucleus is the central region containing protons and neutrons. Electrons occupy the surrounding region.
  • Neutral matter contains charged particles, but their charges cancel overall.
  • Charge is measured in coulombs, symbol C. A is ampere, V volt and Ω ohm.
  • Eight positive and nine negative equal-sized elementary charges give 8 − 9 = −1.
  • Its positive total exceeds its negative total by two elementary charges: 8 − 6 = +2.
  • A neutron is electrically neutral. An ion is an atom or group of atoms with net charge.
  • Micro means one millionth: 1 μC = 10⁻⁶ C. The prefix is different from milli, which is one thousandth.
  • The magnitude of one electron’s charge is about 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Divide 1 C by this magnitude to obtain about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons.
  • Conductivity depends on mobile carriers, not a pre-existing net charge. The positive ion cores and electrons can balance while some electrons remain mobile.

02

Electron transfer and conservation

Understand the ideas

  • The model transfers electrons from cloth to balloon; the positive nuclei stay in their materials.
  • Adding negative charge produces a negative imbalance if the positive total stays unchanged.
  • Losing negative electrons leaves a positive excess. Positive charge does not require protons to move into the cloth.
  • Charge can transfer between the objects while the total of the isolated system stays unchanged.
  • Three negative elementary charges leave one patch and enter the other. The total is still zero.
  • The transferred electrons already exist. The direction depends on the material pair and surface conditions.
  • Static charge can move while an object is charging, redistributing charge or discharging. Static does not mean charge is permanently immobile.
  • Conservation requires the two final charges to add to the initial zero total.
  • Net charge is the signed sum. Removing negative charge leaves an imbalance in favour of the positive total already present.
  • The total remains +1 μC. The other charge must satisfy −2 + Q = +1, so Q = +3 μC.

03

Forces and polarisation

Understand the ideas

  • Like net charges repel: negative–negative and positive–positive.
  • Opposite charge signs attract. A force can act without direct contact.
  • Force is a push or pull. Electrical forces are associated with charge and can act across a space.
  • Charges shift slightly so opposite charge can be nearer an external charged object; the polarised object can remain neutral overall.
  • Polarisation puts opposite charge closer. That attraction can outweigh the more distant like-charge repulsion.
  • Both an oppositely charged object and a neutral polarised object can be attracted.
  • Repulsion provides stronger evidence of like charge. Attraction alone can also occur through polarisation of neutral matter.
  • For the point-charge idealisation, force magnitude is proportional to 1/r². The force arrows in our introductory lab are qualitative.
  • Redistribution changes position, not the signed total, when no charge enters or leaves.
  • Changing distance changes force magnitude without necessarily changing net charge or whether the interaction attracts or repels.

04

Conductors and insulators

Understand the ideas

  • Some electrons in a metal can move through it; the positive ion cores remain part of the lattice.
  • An insulator does not readily conduct charge through its bulk, so a surface imbalance can persist.
  • Copper has mobile electrons. These dry insulating materials do not conduct nearly as readily under ordinary conditions.
  • Metal ion cores occupy positions in a solid lattice and vibrate around those positions.
  • Electronic conduction in metal involves mobile electrons; positive ion cores do not travel along the wire.
  • A wire can have balanced overall charge and still support a directed flow of mobile electrons.
  • The leakage depends on surface and environmental conditions. The point is a possible discharge path, not disappearance of charge.
  • Local contact may discharge a patch, but poor bulk and surface conduction can keep remote charge in place.
  • Net charge is an imbalance; conductivity describes mobility. They are different properties.
  • The metal’s structure holds the ion cores in the solid. Mobile electrons carry the net drift current.

Earthing and discharge

Understand the ideas

  • An earth connection allows charge transfer between the conducting object and the large charge reservoir of Earth.
  • The sphere’s excess electrons can flow through the conducting lead into Earth until the isolated imbalance is removed.
  • The electron deficit can be filled from Earth through the conducting connection.
  • A discharge transfers existing charge. A spark is one visible form of discharge through air.
  • The stand helps the isolated sphere retain charge until the intended lead is attached.
  • Electrical breakdown can make air conduct. Charge transfer through the conducting region can produce light, sound and heating.
  • Bonding provides an electrical link between conducting parts; engineered procedures reduce spark risk. This is not a fuel-handling activity for students.
  • The purposes can overlap in systems, but an inter-object connection and a connection to Earth are distinct ideas.
  • Three excess electrons leave in the simplified selected-patch model, leaving 8 positives and 8 negatives.
  • Lightning paths are more complex than a geometric shortest line; it may occur within clouds as well as to ground.

Static electricity in everyday life

Understand the ideas

  • Toner particles respond to controlled charge patterns, then the image is transferred and fixed.
  • Electrostatic forces can assist deposition, while repulsion between similarly charged droplets can help spread a spray.
  • Charged particles are attracted towards collecting surfaces. This does not guarantee removal of gaseous pollutants.
  • Contact and separation can charge a person; touching a conductor can allow a brief discharge.
  • The charge helps separate suspended material from flowing gas, but collection and later removal are still needed.
  • A defibrillator concerns stored electrical energy and a controlled current pulse; it is not merely electrostatic attraction or a universal restart device.
  • Dry insulating materials can retain charge. Attraction may involve opposite charges or polarisation.
  • Static examples concern a persisting imbalance; a driven circuit can sustain current and energy transfer.
  • Particles and gaseous pollutants are different. A particular separation method addresses specific materials.
  • The same charge concepts underlie both. A battery supplies energy and maintains conditions for flow; rubbing can redistribute existing charge.
