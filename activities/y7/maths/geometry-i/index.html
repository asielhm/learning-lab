Geometry describes position, shape and size. A point marks a position, a line segment joins two points, and an angle describes a turn between directions. Shape names tell you properties, such as equal sides or parallel edges; a drawing that looks convincing is not a proof. Read the stated properties before calculating.

Perimeter counts distance around a boundary, while area counts how many square units cover a region. Splitting a polygon into triangles explains its interior-angle sum: (n − 2) × 180°. Dividing that total by n finds one interior angle only when the polygon is regular. For compound shapes, include every exposed boundary in the perimeter and avoid counting shared internal edges.

Connect it to everyday life and industry

A rectangular garden 4 m by 3 m needs 14 m of fencing but has 12 m² of ground to cover. A 5 m by 2 m garden needs the same fence length and has a different area, 10 m². A builder ordering paving and fencing must distinguish these quantities and units.

01 · Triangles and quadrilaterals: look for properties

Classify shapes using side lengths, parallel sides, angles and symmetry.

A triangle has three sides; a quadrilateral has four. Rotate a drawing mentally: a tilted square is still a square.

Three equal sides and three angles of 60°. Three lines of symmetry.

3 sides · 180° interior angle sum

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A side is a straight boundary segment; a vertex is a corner. A triangle has three sides; a quadrilateral has four. Parallel lines stay the same distance apart; perpendicular lines meet at 90°. Equal sides have equal lengths. A right angle is 90°; acute is less than 90° and obtuse is between 90° and 180°.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Count the sides: 3.
  2. The angle sum is (3 − 2) × 180° = 180°.
  3. Use the stated properties to classify it; the drawing’s orientation does not change the shape.
02 · Area and perimeter: start with a single shape

Use perpendicular height for area and count the boundary for perimeter.

Start with whole-number dimensions and rectangles, then try triangles. Write cm² for area and cm for perimeter.

The area counts square units inside. The perimeter counts the lengths of all four edges.

Area = 24 cm² · perimeter = 20 cm

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

Perimeter P is the distance around the boundary, measured in length units such as cm. Area A measures the surface covered, in square units such as cm². For a rectangle, A = b × h: b is base length and h perpendicular height. For a triangle, A = ½bh: half the area of the matching rectangle. Height must meet the base at a right angle; a sloping side is not usually the height.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Area = base × height = 6 × 4 = 24 cm².
  2. Perimeter = 2 × (6 + 4) = 20 cm.
03 · Build a polygon, then triangulate it

Discover the interior-angle sum and each interior angle of a regular polygon.

Begin with a triangle, quadrilateral and pentagon. Count triangles before using a formula.

Every polygon here is convex. Drawing diagonals from one vertex creates n − 2 triangles. Equal angles are guaranteed only when the polygon is regular.

Sum = 540° · each regular angle = 108°

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A polygon is a closed shape made of straight sides. n is its number of sides. A diagonal joins non-neighbouring vertices. Splitting from one vertex gives n − 2 triangles, so the interior-angle sum is (n − 2) × 180°. A regular polygon has all sides and interior angles equal; each interior angle is that sum divided by n. ° means degrees.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Number of triangles: 5 − 2 = 3.
  2. Interior-angle sum: 3 × 180° = 540°.
  3. For a regular polygon, divide equally: 540 ÷ 5 = 108°.
04 · Parallel lines and a transversal

Use corresponding, alternate and straight-line angles instead of measuring the picture.

Mark an F for corresponding angles or a Z for alternate angles, after checking that the lines are parallel.

Corresponding angles are equal when the lines are parallel. Alternate angles are equal too; co-interior angles add to 180°. Angles on a straight line add to 180° and angles at a point to 360°.

Corresponding = 60° · straight-line partner = 120°

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A transversal is a line crossing two other lines. With parallel lines, corresponding angles occupy matching positions; alternate angles lie on opposite sides of the transversal and are equal; co-interior angles inside the parallels on the same side add to 180°. Angles on a straight line total 180°; angles around a point total 360°.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Find the corresponding position on the second parallel line: 60°.
  2. Its straight-line partner is 180° − 60° = 120°.
  3. Two intersecting lines form alternating angles of 60° and 120°; vertically opposite angles are equal.
05 · Construct a bisector, one step at a time

Use equal compass radii to justify a perpendicular bisector or an angle bisector.

Move through stages 1–4 in order. Keep the compass opening unchanged between matching arcs.

Every point on the constructed line is equally far from A and B. Use a ruler/straightedge and compass on paper; the on-screen construction shows the geometric reason.

Stage 1: Draw segment AB.

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

To bisect means to divide into two equal parts. An angle bisector splits an angle equally. A perpendicular bisector crosses a segment at its midpoint at 90°. A compass draws arcs with a fixed radius, the distance from its point to its pencil; equal-radius arcs construct equal distances rather than estimate them by eye.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Draw segment AB.
  2. Centre the compass at A. Use a radius greater than half of AB and draw an arc above and below.
  3. Keep exactly the same radius. Repeat from B to create two intersections.
  4. Use a straightedge to join the intersections. This line bisects AB at 90°.
06 · Area and perimeter of a compound shape

Separate the space inside a shape from the distance around its boundary.

Use whole-number lengths first. Split the L into two rectangles or subtract the missing rectangle; both methods give the same area.

The dashed corner is removed. Subtract its area from the large rectangle. Walk around every boundary edge for perimeter; do not subtract the missing corner’s perimeter.

Area = 68 cm² · perimeter = 36 cm

Words, symbols and units · unpack the idea

A compound shape combines simpler shapes. Split its area into rectangles or triangles and add their areas, or subtract a missing piece. Use the same length unit throughout. Only exposed outer edges count towards perimeter; an internal dividing line is not part of the outside boundary. Label missing lengths using aligned totals before calculating.

Worked example · follow the reasoning

  1. Outer rectangle: 10 × 8 = 80 cm².
  2. Cut-out: 4 × 3 = 12 cm².
  3. Remaining area: 80 − 12 = 68 cm². You can also add three rectangles: (6 × 3) + (6 × 5) + (4 × 5).
  4. Boundary: (6) + 3 + 4 + (5) + 10 + 8 = 36 cm.
